# Offer and proof

Contents: wording the deal · the reason for it · scarcity and now · gifts and guarantees · believing it · flaws · social proof · who says it · where the books disagree

Before and afters use invented brands. Much of Cialdini's evidence is favours, donations and shop-floor studies, Hormozi's is his own experience, and nearly all of it predates reels. A number below tells you what happened there, not what a reel will do.

## Wording the deal

**Same deal, different words, different sales.**
Default: "25% off" or "3 for R300".
Books: 50% off, half price and buy one get one free are the same deal, and Stone reports the last outpulled the other two by 40% (`bencivenga-0077`). Hormozi says "one for R30, get two free" gets far more attention than "three for R30" at identical cost (`hormozi-money-0143`).
Before (Fynn): "Three pairs, 33% off." After: "Buy one pair, get two free."
Say it as the customer gets it, and only where the client's terms really allow that wording.

**Name it and say what's inside.**
Default: "Get the bundle."
Books: a booklet offer pulling under 50 replies per ad went to over 400 once a free pair of sunglasses and a map were added and it was named a "Free Bermuda Vacation Kit" (`caples-0287`). Spelling out what was in a booklet raised requests over 500% (`caples-0288`). People count items more easily than they judge value (`hormozi-offers-0069`).
Before (Dune Dog): "The training bundle." After: "The Recall Kit: two bags, one whistle, one long lead."

**One thing, one price.**
Default: the full range and every colour.
Books: Sugarman's split run showed one men's watch against nine; the one outsold the nine 3 to 1, though the nine looked better (`sugarman-0205`). A simple one-price offer beat a complicated one (`sugarman-0207`). Short TV wants one offer with no choices (`sugarman-0336`). Given one alternative to studying, 21% of students studied; given two, 40% did: more good options made them less likely to pick any (`heath-0037`).
Reel: one product in shot. The range goes on the site.

**Make the price feel small by moving what it's compared with.**
Default: "worth every cent", or a comparison with a cheaper rival.
Books: don't defend a high price; switch the comparison to a dearer standard (`schwartz-0177`). See the expensive thing first and later prices seem lower (`cialdini-0026`).
Before (Kloof Kettle): "Only R899." After: a R45 flat white goes down on the counter, then another, then another. "Twenty coffees."

**Put big costs in small familiar units.**
Default: "saves you R3,000 a year".
Books: Heath holds up Grant's packet of salt and sugar, priced against a cup of tea, as the model of a number people can feel (`heath-0126`). A cost only becomes usable at a scale people feel (`heath-0149`).
Reel: hold the thing up and say what it costs in coffees or tanks.

**Small discounts may be doing nothing.**
Default: 10% or 15% off because it's a sale.
Books: Hormozi says mild discounts don't get anyone to buy who wouldn't have, only free or massive ones do (`hormozi-offers-0131`, `hormozi-money-0026`), and that's his view, not a test. Sharp's arithmetic: a 10% cut on a 50% margin shaves 20% off the margin (`sharp-0152`). So if the deal is thin, lead with something else in the ad or change what's included rather than the price (`hormozi-money-0238`). We don't choose the client's deal; the skill words the one they've got as strongly as it truly can.

## The reason for it

**A cut without a reason gets a fraction of its power.**
Default: "30% OFF" and nothing else.
Books: price cuts must be justified or you get only a fraction of the sales (`schwartz-0187`). A discount gets believed when it has a reason, like a fire sale (`bencivenga-0029`). Collier spent six paragraphs on why a mill had overstocked before naming the bargain (`schwartz-0188`). Credibility means explaining why a low price is real (`sugarman-0156`).
Before (Tannie Bee): "20% off this week." After: "We dropped a pallet. Dented lids, same honey. 20% off."
The reason has to be true. Say it in the same beat as the deal.

**"Because" works on small asks.**
Default: a bare "shop now".
Books: Langer's copier queue: 60% let someone cut in with a plain request, about 94% with "because I'm in a rush", and a nearly empty reason worked almost as well (`cialdini-0012`, `bencivenga-0032`). That's five pages, not a purchase, so use it on the tap, not as the reason for buying.

## Scarcity and now

**Supply beats time, and news beats both.**
Default: "Limited time only."
Books: across over 6,700 A/B tests on shops, low-stock highlighting ranked first and countdown timers third among features tested (`cialdini-0261`). Customers told of a coming beef shortage bought more than twice as much; told it was exclusive news, six times as much (`cialdini-0277`). Scarcity from other people's demand is the strongest (`cialdini-0288`).
Before (Stoep): "Hurry, ending soon!" After: a warehouse shelf with four boxes on it. A hand takes one. "That's the last of the green."

**Show it running out.**
Default: "limited stock" as a flat state.
Books: people who watched a jar drop from ten cookies to two rated them higher than those who only saw two (`cialdini-0282`).
Reel: the shelf emptying, the count dropping, on camera, only if it's true.

**No stock limit? Sell what they can't get elsewhere.**
If supply is endless, market a feature or combination nobody else has, and what's lost by missing it (`cialdini-0259`). Fake scarcity gets found out.

**Give them the freedom to say no.**
Default: a louder call to action.
Books: across 42 experiments, adding "But you are free to decline" or similar raised compliance, often more than doubling it (`cialdini-0278`).
Before (Moss Mop): "Buy now before it's too late!" After: "It's R199. Your floor, your call."

**Say it's already theirs.**
Default: "Get yours."
Books: people work harder to avoid losing a thing than to gain it (`cialdini-0252`), and wording that gives possession first makes the alternative feel like a loss (`cialdini-0253`). Only use where it's true, such as a code already in their inbox or a reserved list place.

## Gifts and guarantees

**A gift only helps if everyone wants it.**
Default: "free tote with every order".
Books: a free candy on entry made shoppers 42% more likely to buy, and they bought other candy, so it's obligation, not taste (`cialdini-0044`). A gift before the ask beats a bigger reward after it (`cialdini-0038`). Matched to need, yoghurt for hungry diners lifted spend 24% against 12% for an equal-value key ring (`cialdini-0047`). Sugarman warns a bonus part of the audience doesn't want can put them off the main offer, and says to test gift against no gift (`sugarman-0339`). Hopkins tells of a giveaway that cheapened a food while a maker paying retail through a coupon succeeded (`hopkins-0025`).
Reel: pick the extra a stranger would want now. Give it its real rand value (`hormozi-money-0312`).

**Go past the expected guarantee.**
Default: "30-day money back".
Books: changing only the guarantee doubled a mail test (`sugarman-0166`, `sugarman-0167`). A longer trial lowers returns and raises confidence, because the pressure to decide goes (`sugarman-0142`). Check the client will honour it. If they won't, leave it out.

## Believing it

**The claim and its proof arrive together.**
Default: the promise now, reviews at the end.
Books: every big promise meets "yeah, sure", so a claim must never outrun its proof (`bencivenga-0005`). Predict the winning hook by which carries the better proof: Caples's headline naming doctors beat the plain one by 71% (`bencivenga-0016`, `bencivenga-0017`). Proof gets stronger right where the viewer is hungry for it, staged as the answer to a problem you've just made urgent (`schwartz-0213`).
Reel: you should be able to point at the second the claim is shown working.

**An exact figure, not a round one.**
Default: "over 50% stronger", "thousands of customers".
Books: an agency reports that advertising a building material as 52.7% over the government standard was so powerful the trade body asked for the true copy to be toned down (`caples-0156`). Superlatives are discounted as selling talk, figures are taken at face value (`hopkins-0027`).
Before (Fynn): "Thousands of happy hikers." After: "11,482 pairs. 63 sent back."

**One number at most, made into a thing.**
Default: a stack of stats.
Books: in a Stanford exercise 63% recalled the stories and 5% any statistic (`heath-0248`). People keep the relationship, not the figure (`heath-0146`). A popcorn campaign put a day's greasy meals on a table instead of a fat count (`heath-0005`). The same accuracy claim was "very impressive" to 83% as New York to LA, 58% as sun to earth (`heath-0147`).
Before (Salt Road): "SPF 50, 94% blocked." After: two arms, one tan line, shown side by side.

**Show what you turn away.**
Default: "only the finest".
Books: a grapefruit grower listed what he rejects, wind scars and "sheep nose", and that proved the standard (`sugarman-0131`). VW opened on a blemish that got a car rejected and counted 3,389 inspectors (`ogilvy-0227`).
Before (Tannie Bee): "Only the best honey." After: "We tasted this batch. It went back." A full jar slid off the table edge into a bin.

**Let them check it.**
Default: assert the benefit.
Books: a testable claim gives a large credibility boost, because the viewer verifies it (`heath-0161`). Turn the demo into a test they picture doing themselves (`schwartz-0095`). Hormozi says live proof in about a minute sends belief through the roof (`hormozi-leads-0073`). One unbroken take, no cutaway before the result.

**One hard case beats a statistic.**
Default: "98% satisfied".
Books: one example from the hardest case in the field is enough: Sinatra's "if I can make it there" (`heath-0154`). Jared was introduced by name with before and after numbers and an honest "not for everyone"; Subway sales went from flat in 1999 to +18% in 2000, though nothing isolates the ad (`heath-0223`, `heath-0224`).
Reel: the fussiest dog, the worst floor, the man who walks 30 km a day.

## Flaws

**Say the flaw, then the best thing, with no gap.**
Default: hide the weakness and lead with the strength.
Books: "It Tastes Awful. And It Works": the negative must come first (`cialdini-0491`). Sites showing some negative reviews convert 67% higher (`cialdini-0240`). Schwartz says the frank line works by sitting right on top of the claim (`schwartz-0215`), and "we're second, so we try harder" joins two ideas (`schwartz-0214`). Name it early: size, price, delay (`sugarman-0155`).
Before (Stoep): "The comfiest chair on the market." After: "It weighs six kilos. You won't want to get up, though."
Fake flaws ("our only problem is people love it too much") do the opposite.

**The flaw can be the proof.**
Default: edit out the odd bit.
Books: hail-scarred apples were sold as proof of mountain growing, and refund requests hit a record low (`bencivenga-0025`).
Reel: find the quirk the client apologises for and put it on screen as the evidence.

**Too perfect looks fake.**
Default: "5.0 stars".
Books: purchase likelihood peaks at an average of 4.2 to 4.7 stars, and above that shoppers suspect a fake (`cialdini-0239`).
Reel: show the real 4.6, with a real "the lid's fiddly" beside the good one, if the client has them.

**Tell them what not to buy.**
Default: push the biggest size.
Books: unselling means saying what they don't need so they believe you about what they do (`hormozi-money-0205`). It's Hormozi's claim, though it agrees with Cialdini's finding that advice against one's own interest persuades (`cialdini-0244`).

## Social proof

**Name what most people pick.**
Default: "our customers love it".
Books: labelling a dish "most popular" lifted sales 13% to 20% at no cost (`cialdini-0133`). "The McFlurry is our visitors' favourite" lifted it 55% (`cialdini-0134`). A young brand can use a rising number, since people follow where a trend is heading (`cialdini-0196`).
Before (Dune Dog): "Loved by dogs." After: "The liver one sells three to one. Nobody knows why."

**Let proof arrive by accident.**
Default: "everyone's buying it, get yours".
Books: social proof persuades more when it doesn't seem meant to (`cialdini-0140`).
Reel: boxes piling up behind someone mid-sentence. Don't mention them.

**Many people in the proof shot.**
Default: one model.
Books: a poster of several people at early lunch lifted early traffic 75%; one person, 25% (`cialdini-0164`). It works hardest when people are unsure of their preference: dessert yes, burger no (`cialdini-0480`). So spend it on what they have no view on.

## Who says it

**A liked face over an expert, for feelings.**
Default: a doctor in a white coat.
Books: liked celebrities moved opinion on evolution; a prestigious professor didn't significantly (`cialdini-0473`). An ordinary person who's lived it has more credibility than the expert (`heath-0136`). A friend with no stake earns more trust than a company (`heath-0138`).
Reel: for a real star, see below.

**Testimony needs a question to answer.**
Default: "I love this product".
Books: ask what the problem was, how it felt, what was different, the moment it worked, what life's like now (`miller-0159`). Then cut it. Names, places, days make it real (`heath-0139`). Leave the rough edges (`ogilvy-0100`).

## Where the books disagree

- **Stars.** Ogilvy says celebrities are remembered and the product forgotten (`ogilvy-0084`); Cialdini's liked-messenger finding says they move opinion (`cialdini-0473`). We lean: a star is fine when the idea uses what the star is, and the sound-off test still has to pass for a stranger.
- **Free in the hook.** Caples found free in the headline lifts response (`caples-0188`) but warns those leads may be poorer, so count sales (`caples-0283`); Hopkins has a case of free cheapening a food (`hopkins-0025`). Hormozi says free beats a one-cent price (`hormozi-money-0019`). We lean: a named gift with a real value, judged on purchases.
- **Anchor size.** Hormozi wants a premium at 5 to 10 times (`hormozi-money-0218`); Cialdini's first ask backfires if it looks unreasonable (`cialdini-0061`). We lean: two or three times, real.
- **Specific promise.** Sugarman warns that a measurable promise kills hope when it fails (`sugarman-0227`); nearly everyone else says numbers. We lean: count what can be counted on screen, let customers describe what varies from person to person.
- **Guarantee early or late.** Sugarman found it worked at the very end (`sugarman-0167`); others want it early (`masters-0066`). We lean: said right after the claim, if there's time; a 15-second ad usually has none.
- **Discount or value first.** Sharp shows a cut sells much of its volume to people who'd have paid full price (`sharp-0153`), and repeated deals lower the price people expect (`sharp-0147`); Caples's bargain ads with exact prices drew three times the customers (`caples-0083`). We lean: value first for strangers; price up front for warm viewers.
