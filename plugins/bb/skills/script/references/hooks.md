# Hooks: the first two seconds

Contents: what reels actually do · the kinds · what the books found · six to eight, keep three · the sound-off test · where the books disagree · what went wrong before

Before and afters use invented brands.

## What reels actually do

The reels we pick as references work on two things and few words: **a text line on the first frame and a picture you get on mute.** The camera is often a character: in one, two men take turns throwing a ball straight at the lens, so the viewer is the other player. The end gag can be a look, not a line: the ball goes in the pool, two blank faces stare at the water, nobody says anything, nobody falls in.

No book tested a muted feed, so this comes from watching what works. The nearest evidence: burn the promise in as type, because it raises sales (`ogilvy-0107`), and make picture and headline tell the same story, because pictures convey their message faster than print (`caples-0205`).

Copy the grammar, not the gag. Not every idea is a talking head, and not every idea is someone falling in a pool. The question is always what picture, in this brand's world, a stranger gets on mute.

## The kinds

Six to eight hooks for an ad spread across these. Tag each kept hook `<!-- kind: x -->` under the Hook label.

| Kind | What it is | Reads on mute as |
|---|---|---|
| `result` | The end state first: the after, the product doing its thing. | The payoff, then why. |
| `odd-picture` | Something that doesn't belong, but only this product explains it. | "What is going on?" |
| `camera` | The lens is a character: thrown at, handed things, told off, ignored. | "That's me." |
| `reaction` | Faces reacting; the thing they're reacting to isn't shown yet. | "What did they see?" |
| `flaw` | The brand attacks its own product. | "Why would they say that?" |
| `viewer` | Their own moment or attitude, so the right people recognise it. Best for cold. | A line that names them. |
| `news` | Something new and true: a launch, a last run, a first, a number. | The news as text. |
| `deal-first` | The offer is the hook. For warm and most-aware viewers. | The deal on screen. |
| `dare` | A challenge: catch, try, guess, bet. | The challenge as text. |
| `pov` | "POV:" text puts the viewer in a situation. | Fully readable. |
| `skit` | Cold open mid-scene with more than one person. | Faces and action. |
| `statement` | One person to camera, saying a line. The default the model drifts to; one in a pack is plenty. | Often nothing. |

## What the books found

**Offer or news beats a tease.**
Default: "You won't believe what this does."
Books: a self-interest headline brought twice the inquiries and sales of a curiosity one, partly because it delivers something to people who only catch the big print (`caples-0021`). Ten outright failures were curiosity-only teasers with no news or benefit, and most were gloomy (`caples-0037`, `caples-0038`).
Before (Fynn socks): "Wait till you see this." After: foot comes out of a river, sock already dry to the touch. On screen: "Dry 40 minutes after the river."

**Start where it gets going.**
Default: a warm-up sentence the writer needed and the viewer doesn't.
Books: copy chiefs improve copy by pointing halfway down the page and saying "begin here" (`caples-0113`).
Before (Moss Mop): "So I've been using this for a few weeks now and honestly..." After: delete until the first line a stranger would care about. Usually line three.

**The picture has to be literally about the product.**
Default: a mood shot or a metaphor.
Books: a poster for a radio showed a motorboat; a man on the bus asked what kind of motorboat it was (`caples-0214`). Pictures that sell show the product, the product in use, the reward, or the ambition reached (`caples-0231`).
Reel: odd is fine, but the odd thing has to be this product, or what it does.

**Oddness has to be paid off by the product.**
Default: a shock to stop the scroll.
Books: nearly everyone asked why the Hathaway man wore an eye patch and read on (`caples-0357`). Give the viewer something never seen, a visual burr (`ogilvy-0107`). But surprise with no resolution is frustration, not stickiness: PHRAUG makes you smile once you see it, HENSION just makes you frown (`heath-0069`). A surprise must make sense in hindsight (`heath-0070`). A gorilla in a jockstrap would score high on recall (`ogilvy-0153`), so being remembered proves nothing on its own.
Before (Tannie Bee): a man in a bee suit dances in a car park. After: a man in a bee suit reading the back of a supermarket honey jar, slowly shaking his head.

**Show the reaction, hold back the thing.**
Default: open on the product close-up, then the benefit.
Books: Sugarman filmed ordinary people on the street trying BluBlocker sunglasses and deliberately never showed the view through them. Almost 8 million pairs, six years of commercials (`sugarman-0193`).
Before (Bergwind): biltong on a board, then a person eating it. After: three people at a braai stop talking and look at something below the frame. On screen: "Nobody's sharing this one."

**Attack your own product first.**
Default: praise.
Books: Sugarman opened one ad "Magic Baloney... we're going to tear it apart", then reversed it (`sugarman-0054`), and put the biggest worry first (`sugarman-0277`). "It Tastes Awful. And It Works" worked; a tested finding is that the negative only helps when it comes first (`cialdini-0491`).
Before (Stoep): "The comfiest chair you'll own." After: on screen: "It's ugly. Everyone will tell you." Person sits. Doesn't get up for the rest of the ad.

**For sceptical markets, agree before you promise.**
Default: open on the biggest claim.
Books: a TV-repair ad flopped on "Save up to $100". The rewrite opened "Why haven't TV owners been told these facts?", no claim, just the owner's own suspicion, and built yes after yes before any promise (`schwartz-0150`, `schwartz-0151`). A claim that can't be believed loses the viewer, so test a softer opener (`schwartz-0148`).
Before (Salt Road): "The best SPF on the market." After: on screen: "Why does every sun cream sting your eyes?"

**Small condition, big promise.**
Default: "Lose weight fast!"
Books: "If you've got 20 minutes a month, I guarantee..." beat strong controls on two products. Admitting a little effort is more believable than none (`bencivenga-0006`, `bencivenga-0007`).
Before (Kloof Kettle): "Perfect coffee every time." After: "If you can press one button, you can stop paying R45 a cup."

**Let the line fight the picture.**
Default: line and picture say the same pleasant thing.
Books: Volkswagen headlines pulled people into the copy by seeming to contradict the photograph above them, so you had to read on to resolve it (`schwartz-0117`).
Before (Dune Dog): a dog eating, "Dogs love it." After: a dog staring at an untouched bowl. On screen: "Best thing we ever made."

**Say who, say easy.**
Default: a bare topic line.
Books: "Car owners!" added to the same headline pulled 20% more (`caples-0216`); changing "Repair" to "Fix" in a title raised orders 20% (`caples-0218`). Hormozi's four openers: a label, a yes question, an if-then, a ridiculous result (`hormozi-leads-0178`). Show the label too: cast and set-dress like the buyer (`hormozi-leads-0180`).

**The problem is one person, the answer is a crowd.**
Default: "Everyone struggles with this."
Books: a sign showing three thieves nearly tripled theft at the Petrified Forest, to 7.92%; a lone thief cut it to 1.67%, against about 2.95% with neither (`cialdini-0194`). Dramatising how common a problem is makes it normal.
Before (Moss Mop): "We all hate dirty floors." After: one man, one very bad floor, alone. The crowd only appears at the fix.

**Tell them to wait.**
Default: "shop now" energy.
Books: "Buy no desk until..." stopped readers by saying the opposite of every other ad (`caples-0055`). "Men who know it all are not invited to read this page" worked, and Caples guesses it was the strangeness (`caples-0129`).
Before (Stoep): "Get yours today." After: on screen: "Don't buy a camp chair until you've sat in this one."

**Point at something.**
Default: "Are you making mistakes with your dog?"
Books: "Do you make these mistakes in English?" beat "Are you afraid of making mistakes in English?" on the same copy (`caples-0018`). "These" promises something concrete. Say the count, then deliver every one (`hormozi-leads-0097`).

**Let them guess wrong.**
Default: state the fact.
Books: asking "which kills more people?" first lets people get it wrong and discover it themselves, which teaches better than telling (`heath-0164`).
Reel: one on-screen guess; the next shot shows they were wrong. It has to pay off in the clip.

**For odd ones, start from a template.**
Default: a blank page, then the first joke that comes.
Books: of 200 award-winning ads, 89% fit six templates against 2% of 200 non-winners, and two hours of training on them made novices' ads better (`heath-0021`, `heath-0022`, `heath-0024`). Those are awards, not sales, so use them to get ideas, not to judge them.
Reel: push the product's point to an extreme consequence or an extreme situation, then film the plain version.

**Make the thing you want seen the only thing that moves.**
Default: a busy frame.
Books: the eye goes first to movement, then the brightest area, then eyes, then whatever contrasts most (`block-0113`).
Reel: one mover, brightest thing is the product or face.

**When an ad is tired, change the first seconds only.**
Default: rewrite the script.
Books: Colgate kept one selling message and changed only a startling opening demonstration (`schwartz-0114`). Hormozi reports re-cutting only a flop's first five seconds took it from 4,000 to 850,000 views (`hormozi-leads-0040`), his anecdote, not a test. He changes creative before copy, and keeps the offer (`hormozi-offers-0129`).

## Six to eight, keep three

1. Write six to eight per ad across at least four kinds. For each, say how likely a model would be to write it, and keep going until at least two feel unlikely: ones you'd hesitate to show the client. If two would be filmed the same way, they're one hook.
2. One of the six is plain and direct, whatever you think of it, because plain tends to beat clever where it's been tested (`caples-0221`).
3. For each: first frame, on-screen line, spoken line or none, the mute reading, the passage. All in `reasoning.md` §3.
4. Keep three: one safe, one odd, and a third that doesn't say the deal. Whichever hook the edit picks still runs into the close, so a deal-first spare means the deal said twice. The best becomes the hook on the page; the other two go under "Other hooks to film", written as do and say.
5. Across the pack, no two ads open the same way and not every hook leans on the star. Hooks share one body, so each must land on the same first beat with no dangling "it" or "this".

## The sound-off test

Only the first frame, the movement and the words on screen. Would a stranger know something is happening, and roughly what? Would they know whose ad it is in three seconds? Would it still work if they didn't know the star or the category's own problem (a dare to "spot the pilling" only lands if you know cheap jerseys pill)? A whispered first line fails by definition.

## Where the books disagree

- **Should the opening carry the benefit?** Caples and Ogilvy say the line must, since most see nothing else (`caples-0021`, `ogilvy-0074`). Sugarman and Schwartz say the first line only earns the next one, and pitching first tested worse for him (`sugarman-0039`, `schwartz-0026`). We lean: the on-screen line carries the appeal, because that's all a muted viewer gets. The spoken line can be Sugarman-short.
- **Clever or odd?** Plain beat clever in tests (`caples-0221`); oddness pulled when it carried the point (`masters-0053`, `caples-0357`). We lean: odd pictures yes, wordplay no. Keep one plain line as well.
- **Line length.** Sugarman wants a first sentence so short it barely counts (`sugarman-0037`); Caples found adding words lifted replies (`caples-0217`). We lean, and this number is ours: three to six words for the big text, while the spoken line carries the whole promise.
- **Curiosity as engine.** Heath treats the gap as what holds people (`heath-0084`); Caples's failures were bare teasers (`caples-0037`). We lean: a gap plus something the viewer gets.

## What went wrong before

From the first baseline pack, patterns not facts: five hooks of one kind, all spoken and gentle, only one surviving sound-off; hooks that needed you to know the star; the most-worn opener on Reels ("I've got some sad news"); a strong stranger-stopping picture available and barely used; the best hook was the riskiest shot. Keep risky shots, and write the safe way to film them.
