# Body and close

Contents: beats by awareness · keep them moving · shapes that work · what to leave unsaid · who gets the screen time · skits · directable · how long · the close · where the books disagree

Before and afters use invented brands. Most of this evidence is long copy and film; the translation to 15 to 60 seconds is ours.

## Beats by awareness

- **Unaware:** hook describes them, then the turn (the product enters as the answer to the scene), one proof shown, close. The name arrives late. `schwartz-0036`
- **Problem aware:** the need, felt; the product as the fix; the one proof that matters; close. `schwartz-0033`
- **Solution aware:** name the result, show it can happen, show the product is how, close. `schwartz-0031`
- **Product aware:** the thing they don't believe yet, proved; close. `schwartz-0029`
- **Most aware:** the deal is most of the ad; one reason to believe the price (`schwartz-0187`); close. `schwartz-0028`

## Keep them moving

**Every beat has to sell the next one.**
Default: a tidy list of points, each complete.
Books: if a reader gets through the first quarter they usually finish (`sugarman-0054`), and each element must pull to the next like a greased slide (`sugarman-0052`). A good script makes every scene a turn that opens a new question (`heath-0082`).
Reel: read it beat by beat and ask what makes the viewer need the next one. If the answer is nothing, cut it. Close every loop you open, because loops that never pay teach people not to come back (`hormozi-leads-0099`).

**Switch before they get bored.**
Default: claim, claim, claim, then proof at the end.
Books: the viewer keeps asking in turn "who says?" and "how does it work?", and the writer must switch to proof or mechanism at the exact moment they ask (`schwartz-0181`). Plan the ad as a sequence of reactions (`schwartz-0147`).
Reel: mark on the draft where a stranger thinks "says who?" The next shot answers it.

**Earn the bold claim.**
Default: the biggest claim in line one.
Books: the same claim thrown away at the top is believed in the middle, once the reader has been prepared (`masters-0060`). Start from something accepted (`masters-0130`).
Before (Salt Road): "Lasts all day, guaranteed" in frame one. After: frame one, a lifeguard reapplying nothing at 4 pm; the claim lands after you've seen it.

**Slip the hardest claim in with the easy ones.**
Default: announce it with a card and a sound effect.
Books: Schwartz's TV-repair ad introduces its least believable claim as simply another in the series: same paragraph shape, led by "And, most important", no subhead. The novelty is deliberately hidden (`schwartz-0155`).
Before (Fynn): "AND THE BIG ONE: lasts ten years!" After: "Dries fast. Doesn't smell. And they'll outlast your boots." Same rhythm, same shot size, no pause.

## Shapes that work

**The failed history.** Default: a generic before and after. When the viewer knows a recurring bad experience, run it in order, tried, tried, back again, and only then bring the product in (`schwartz-0196`).
Before (Dune Dog): "Sick of treats that don't work?" After: on screen "Week 1: liver." Dog walks off. "Week 2: cheese." Dog walks off. "Week 3:" The red bag. Dog sprints at the lens.

**Heaven or hell, same frame.** Default: one loose problem line, then the product. Paint the problem in full, then the answer in parallel, so you get two motivations: away from the old and towards the new (`schwartz-0102`). Cut bad and good in the same framing with the same line shape.

**The catalogue close.** Default: benefits scattered all the way through. Build one desire, then in the last seconds hedge with a fast list of every other use (`schwartz-0107`). Three or four quick shots, one beat each, right before the close, only if the ad is long enough to have built the desire.

**One desire, many pictures of it.** Default: a new message per scene. Build one desire and draw it in as many concrete images as the time allows, rather than adding messages (`masters-0126`, `schwartz-0087`).

**Say the thing like one person to a friend.** Default: brand voice. Ogilvy's Dove ad is a woman gushing about her bath down the phone, followed by a plain note with the facts and the money-back line (`ogilvy-0226`). Rough delivery helps, because amateur-sounding users are more credible than polished performers (`ogilvy-0100`).

## Leave it unsaid

**Make them work a little.**
Default: spell out the point, then the moral.
Books: the more the mind works to reach a conclusion it does reach, the more it enjoys it (`sugarman-0214`). Sugarman left the jewellers' mark-up for readers to work out, and believed that was stronger (`sugarman-0215`). Heath argues NBA rookies caught out by a staged moment would remember it better than the NFL's condom-on-a-banana lecture (`heath-0166`).
Reel: let a look or a shot do the punchline. A line that names the gag flattens it.

**One shot can replace a page.**
Default: a line explaining what we just saw.
Books: moving images often carry what a script spends pages explaining (`pearlman-0133`), and once a beat has landed, repeating it dulls it (`pearlman-0134`).
Reel: if the Do shows it, delete the Say.

## Who gets the screen time

**Spend it on the stakes, not the demo.**
Default: most of the ad is the product working.
Books: in Gallipoli's last 4½ minutes, the run that decides everything gets seven short shots; the rest is the men who'll die if it fails (`pearlman-0187`). In Terminator 2's chase, about one shot in four is close enough to read the boy's face, and identification sells it (`pearlman-0247`).
Reel: about one shot in four is a face you can read. Name the person at stake: the kid, the partner, the mate who said it wouldn't work.

**Bring in the neighbours.**
Default: a solo demo.
Books: an ad for a multi-blossom plant said the neighbours come to look, they take blooms home, you become the street's gardening expert. It sold so many it exhausted nurseries (`masters-0078`). A status benefit comes from how others treat you (`hormozi-leads-0184`).
Reel: the payoff is someone else's face.

**Mark the turn by breaking the pattern.**
Default: even pacing throughout.
Books: changing the shot pattern marks the change of feeling, and it matters that it changes, not how (`pearlman-0221`).
Reel: same shot size through the set-up, then one tight close-up or a burst of cuts at the turn. Write it into the Do.

**Don't stack levers.**
Default: scarcity, social proof, authority, a guarantee and fear in one 30 seconds.
Books: shoehorning several tactics into one message can alert people to the attempt and backfire (`cialdini-0487`). Too many warnings make people resist (`miller-0092`).
Reel: one or two levers per ad. Keep the others for other ads.

## Skits

Funny skits are welcome: a little awkward, a little funny. Every one still ends on one deal, and the joke has to point the same way as the deal.

**The joke is the selling point.**
Default: a funny bit, then a card with the offer.
Books: Arrow's talking-horse ad is a joke about a shirt that doesn't shrink (`caples-0142`). Federal Express's jokes all arrive at overnight (`ogilvy-0128`). Miller Lite's jokes got plenty of attention but didn't build the brand, and sales kept falling (`sharp-0193`).
Test: retell the joke to someone. If you can tell it without the product and the deal, rewrite it.
Before (Bergwind): two men fight over a chair at a braai, then "20% off this weekend". After: two men fight over the last stick of biltong in total silence while a third opens a second bag behind them. Close: "Two bags, R199."

**Simple beats busy.** Before adding a location, a person or a prop, ask what the joke loses without it. The plain ad won our first blind test: one table, one person doing one small thing again and again, tension building by repetition, the close answering the gag with the deal. The ad it beat needed a braai, a sprint, two mates, a second location and sound on. The joke in an action you can see on mute, nothing to set up, room for more takes.

**Cast more than one when the idea is about two.** Pairs, mates, family. One person and an off-camera voice doing everything is a cast of one.

**The end gag can be a look.** Don't reach for a final line because the page has room for one. The deal still lands, on screen or from someone on camera, in the same breath as the look.

## Directable

The director and the performer are on set with a phone. Every beat is "Do this. Do this. Say this." A Do is something a phone can see; anything else gets cut, because concrete means specific people doing specific things (`heath-0104`). A Say is short enough to say in one take, and they can say it their way except the numbers and the close. Write the safe way to film any risky shot.

"Say: nothing" is fine for one beat. Two or more silent beats written into a talky ad, or a timed hold ("waits two seconds"), is dead air; a reel built on picture and on-screen words is different, and there the words on screen are the voice.

## How long

2.5 spoken words a second, and actions take time too.

| Length | Spoken words | Room for |
|---|---|---|
| 8 to 12 s | 20 to 30 | hook, one beat, close |
| 15 s | about 35 | hook, a turn, close; no separate proof |
| 20 s | about 50 | hook, two beats, one proof, close |
| 30 s | about 75 | hook, the belief gap, proof, one flaw, close |
| 60 s | about 150 | the full argument; a story needs this length to have stakes |

Length follows doubt and price. A reducing belt sold in a small ad because a picture explained it; a hair treatment needed a full page, because scepticism takes explanation and testimony (`caples-0248`). The dearer the thing against what the buyer earns, the longer the sell (`hormozi-money-0167`). People don't quit because it's long, they quit because it's boring (`hormozi-leads-0100`).

**Choose: ad or content.** A mail-order ad set as an article pulled 81% more orders than the same words set as an ad, though headline-only readers may never learn what's for sale (`caples-0244`). You can do either but not both: ad tricks in an editorial-style ad just tell people it's an ad (`caples-0252`). So if it's a phone-shot reel, no price bursts over it until the close.

## The close

One deal, said once, exactly, with the price or how, twelve words or fewer.

- The body may **show** the deal. It doesn't **say** it. The commonest miss: the last beat says it and the close says it again.
- One deal per ad. If two fit, it's two ads.
- One message. When the deal is access (a list, a drop, first pick), the close is how to get in; a price on top is a second message.
- One true reason to buy now. Settled lines the client has confirmed are used as written.
- Closes in a pack don't all end the same way.

Why once, when the books say repeat? See below. How to word the deal, its reason and the guarantee is in `offer-and-proof.md`.

## Where the books disagree

- **One claim or many?** Hopkins and Caples say tell more, since the more you tell the more you sell (`hopkins-0030`, `caples-0123`, `caples-0370`). Schwartz, Heath and Masters say one fully believed claim outsells a pile (`schwartz-0158`, `heath-0034`, `masters-0129`). We lean one claim: mail-order readers had chosen to read; a muted scroller hasn't. Fill it with facts that serve that claim.
- **Repeat or once?** Ogilvy's P&G pattern repeats the promise at the end (`ogilvy-0145`), and Sugarman summarises the offer (`sugarman-0146`). Our rule is the deal said once, from Tian's reviews (he owns the taste here) and the blind test, not from a book. The promise can come back as a picture.
- **Humour.** Caples says use it sparingly (`caples-0142`); Hopkins says don't try to amuse (`hopkins-0009`); Ogilvy says it has its place (`ogilvy-0128`). We lean to fun only when the brief asks for it, with the joke as the benefit and a plain hook filmed alongside. An ad is meant to sell first.
- **Short or long?** Direct-response advertisers, who can count, tend to run longer (`ogilvy-0019`). Heath, Murch and Pearlman say cut. We lean to the shortest that carries the doubt, and run the long cut when the product is sceptical or dear.
