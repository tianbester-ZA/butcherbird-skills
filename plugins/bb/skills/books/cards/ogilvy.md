# Ogilvy on Advertising
**David Ogilvy.** Read in full, 239 passages.

## The core idea
An ad is judged by what it sells, and what sells can be learned from measured results rather than taste. Homework finds the right promise, and the promise matters more than the craft around it. Late in the book the case hardens: most campaigns give too little information, advertising mostly reinforces existing users, and the sample ads show that hard facts can carry wit and voice.

## What this changes
- A generic hook teases and reveals later. We say what the product is or does straight away, and open with the fire.
- A generic edit lays voice-over and music over quick B-roll. We start with a person talking on camera, real sound and fewer scenes, then test against faster cuts.
- Generic captions paraphrase the speaker. Our promise text matches the spoken words exactly.
- A generic script uses adjectives and tidy delivery. We use exact counts, the price, a guarantee and a real customer left rough.
- A generic process polishes one version. We test different appeals first, because the wrong one can sink a well-made ad.

## The ten best passages
- `ogilvy-0004` · How to produce advertising that sells · The appeal can matter far more than the craft, and in Caples's example one ad sold 19½ times another · Write separate cuts on price, result, status and relief before polishing any of them
- `ogilvy-0076` · Print advertising · Openers that hide the product lose attention, and specifics beat vague claims · Never make the viewer wait to learn what is being sold; if the opener is odd, name the product or its result in the first line
- `ogilvy-0100` · TV commercials · Polish reduces belief, and an unusual real person can carry a campaign · Cast loyal users, keep the stumbles, and build in one striking demonstration
- `ogilvy-0106` · TV commercials · On camera holds better than voice-over; background music adds no measurable selling power, while real sound can · Start with the person speaking to camera, and build the sound around pouring, sizzling or opening the pack
- `ogilvy-0107` · TV commercials · Supers reinforce the promise only if they match the speech, and lots of short scenes score below average · Burn the promise in word for word, and cut when the thought changes rather than on a timer
- `ogilvy-0138` · Direct response section · Selling direct needs a fuller case than brand work, and Ogilvy says it runs counter to the Procter & Gamble formula · In a direct-sell spot, stack several benefits, show the fix, and give the guarantee, price and urgent ask
- `ogilvy-0145` · Competing with Procter & Gamble · Every P&G commercial has a moment where someone confirms the claim on screen · Plan the shot where someone tries it and reacts or says it worked, then end by repeating the promise
- `ogilvy-0168` · Marketing · Ehrenberg and Treasure argue that ads mostly reinforce users rather than convert strangers · Make the first frame earn a stranger's attention, and write separate reorder and new-use cuts for existing buyers
- `ogilvy-0214` · What's wrong with advertising? · Ogilvy learned to sell on the doorstep, where facts closed the sale · Write the body as the facts a good salesperson would give, and cut any line that is only mood
- `ogilvy-0227` · Sample ads · The Volkswagen ad turns a tiny flaw and exact counts into proof of quality · Open on something rejected, show the real check with its true numbers, and end on one short line

## Three questions for the critic
- Does the first line say what the product is or what it does for the viewer? (`ogilvy-0076`)
- Is there a moment where someone on screen visibly confirms the promise? (`ogilvy-0145`)
- Does the on-screen promise use exactly the same words as the speech? (`ogilvy-0107`)

## What doesn't carry over
- **The numbers:** The recall and readership figures come from print and TV decades ago. Treat them as starting bets to test.
- **Length:** The two-minute rule and 20 seconds of ordering details don't fit this format. Keep the lesson: don't assume shorter sells, and always make the ask clear.
- **Scene length:** The finding comes from TV, not a thumb-scrolled feed. Use it as the starting point, then test it.
- **Brand name timing:** Ten seconds is too late in a 15 second cut. Say or show the brand much earlier.
- **Music and voice-over:** The research predates feeds and muted viewing. Keep captions on, and don't use the book to ban platform-native sound.
- **Print rules:** Serif type, drop initials, columns, coupons and all-caps findings stay on paper. Black on white needs testing too, as Ogilvy himself exempts slides projected on a screen (`ogilvy-0136`).
- **Media timing:** Late-night slots, dull programmes and the best months for TV don't transfer.
- **Celebrities:** Paid stars are not the same as today's creators. A creator who looks and talks like a customer is closer to his testimonial, if the use is real.
- **Made-up proof:** Ogilvy wrote his own anonymous diplomat testimonial (`ogilvy-0083`). Don't copy that: testimonials must be real, and any limited supply claim must be true (`ogilvy-0136`).
- **Voice:** The housewife framing and the Dove character's "girliest girl" are of their time. Keep the structure, not the voice.
