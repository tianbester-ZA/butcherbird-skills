# Building a StoryBrand
**Donald Miller.** Read in full, 173 passages.

## The core idea
People ignore anything that makes them work or doesn't help them survive and thrive, so the customer must be the hero and the brand the guide. The story runs from a want through a problem, a plan and an ask to one clear ending. Late on, the argument moves from what people get to who they become, then shows that change through real customers.

## What this changes
- A generic script says "Tired of X? Meet Y", lists features and ends on a packshot. We name one real villain and the feeling it causes.
- A generic script sells the product. We can sell the irritation it removes and show someone skipping it.
- A generic script uses the category's words. We use what the viewer can picture.
- A generic script ends on "feel your best". We end on one specific, visible change, or on the person the buyer wants to be.
- A generic testimonial says "love this". We ask for the moment it worked and what life looks like now.

## The ten best passages
1. `miller-0008` · Ch 1 · Decoding costs energy, so viewers leave unless they get something useful quickly. · Cut the opening until nothing needs explaining.
2. `miller-0022` · Ch 2 · Plain, picturable words beat expert words. · Swap every trade term for what the viewer sees or gets.
3. `miller-0047` · Ch 5 · A feeling isn't a villain; its root cause is. · Cast one real, relatable cause as a person or object, then let the product beat it.
4. `miller-0052` · Ch 5 · The irritation removed can be the whole ad. · Show someone skipping the annoying part: returns, sizing, waiting.
5. `miller-0069` · Ch 7 · Unanswered worries become reasons not to buy. · If buying looks hard or risky, show the short path before the ask.
6. `miller-0090` · Ch 9 · Too much fear or too little changes nothing; a moderate dose works. · Run threat, reason to act, specific fix, ask, and keep the fear to a pinch.
7. `miller-0094` · Ch 10 · A fuzzy ending excites nobody. · Fill in a before and after, then write the payoff as one visible change.
8. `miller-0105` · Ch 11 · People buy the kind of person a product says they are. · Show that person in action and speak the hook to them.
9. `miller-0115` · Ch 12 · One short promise can do the job of a whole explanation. · Make the first line and first caption one bold statement of what the viewer gets.
10. `miller-0159` · Ch 13 · The right questions turn praise into a transformation story. · Interview in order: problem, frustration, difference, the moment, life now. Then cut with B-roll.

## Three questions for the critic
1. After one watch, could a stranger say what this is, how it improves their life and what to do next? (`miller-0021`)
2. Does every beat move the viewer closer to, or further from, one named bad outcome? (`miller-0086`)
3. Does the ending show one specific, visible change rather than a lofty benefit? (`miller-0094`)

## What doesn't carry over
- **All seven parts in every video.** They won't fit in 15 seconds. Use the chords that serve the one idea, and drop the plan when buying is obvious.
- **Five seconds for everything.** That test is framed around a glance at a website or other marketing material. In a reel, the hook gets the first seconds and the rest can land by the end card.
- **Page and funnel advice.** Above-the-fold layout, buttons, email sequences, lead magnets and referral systems don't apply, beyond a softer second ask.
- **Three testimonials and award logos.** That is website dosing. A short video usually has room for one strong result.
- **The science as fact.** Burning calories is a useful metaphor, and the revenue stories are the author's own anecdotes. Don't treat either as proof.
- **The founder rule, taken literally.** Founder footage can still work if the founder is the guide who knows the viewer's problem and the viewer's win stays the point.
- **Pace, sound and feel.** The book says almost nothing about them. Take only the message cuts from here, and get the craft of the edit elsewhere.
- **Staff and culture chapters.** They have no use for this skill.
