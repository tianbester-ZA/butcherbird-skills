# The Adweek Copywriting Handbook
**Joseph Sugarman.** Read in full, 373 passages.

## The core idea
An ad is a salesperson's conversation on paper: every element exists to get the next line consumed, and the buying feeling is built before the logic arrives. The middle of the book adds the psychological reasons people buy. The late chapters take real ads apart and show how much rests on the opening and the close, including saying the embarrassing thing out loud. They then carry the same rules into other media, and the shorter the format, the stricter they get: count every word and make one offer.

## What this changes
- A generic script opens on the benefit. Here the first lines only earn the next line, and the pitch waits.
- A generic script praises the product. Here it says the worst thing about the product first, then answers it.
- A generic script claims quality. Here it shows what the maker throws away.
- A generic script spells out the takeaway. Here the viewer is left to reach it.
- A generic edit shows the full range and adds extras. Here it shows one product at one price, and anything not leading to the sale gets cut.

## The ten best passages
1. `sugarman-0039` · The First Sentence · He tried leading with the pitch and could not prove his theory wrong. · Keep the first two lines free of features, benefits and offer.
2. `sugarman-0054` · Slippery slide section · Attacking your own product can start the reader down the slide. · Open on a real flaw and what put you off, then show what changed your mind.
3. `sugarman-0078` · Emotion in Advertising · Rivals explained the specs and failed, while a stressful moment sold the same kind of product. · Open a useful product on a moment of pressure, with people watching, then bring it out.
4. `sugarman-0146` · Copy elements · Saying too much is the biggest mistake his students make, and heavy cuts lose little. · Cut the script hard, often by half or more, then end on one line with what they get and the price.
5. `sugarman-0193` · Psychological triggers · Strangers' reactions with the view held back sold almost 8 million pairs. · Show the product and the reactions, but hold back the experience or result.
6. `sugarman-0215` · Psychological triggers · A conclusion the viewer reaches alone is stronger than one you state. · Put the two prices side by side and cut the line that explains them.
7. `sugarman-0233` · Selling a Cure, Not Prevention · A product that does both should sell the fix. · Build the hook on the problem it fixes today, and keep prevention in the background.
8. `sugarman-0259` · A Fluke of Nature · Fussy rejection rules prove quality and put the maker in the viewer's mind. · Film the maker testing and discarding product, and name the exact fault.
9. `sugarman-0274` · The More You Learn · Good reassurance can read as a cover-up if the real issue goes unnamed. · If a brand has a known doubt, such as bad reviews or a past failure, say it first and then answer it.
10. `sugarman-0338` · Writing for Different Media · In a short spot, words and shots are budgeted against the sale. · Check every line and shot against the core message, and set the scene where the product is really used.

## Three questions for the critic
1. Are the first two lines free of any feature, benefit or offer? (`sugarman-0039`)
2. Does the script say the viewer's biggest doubt out loud and answer it straight after? (`sugarman-0274`)
3. Is there at least one conclusion the viewer reaches without the script saying it? (`sugarman-0215`)

## What doesn't carry over
- **Long copy:** "as long as it needs" and the 25 percent read-through figure come from print. A 15 to 60 second video can't borrow either.
- **Slow openings:** long stories and unrelated news clippings take too long. Keep one moment and link it to the product quickly.
- **Stock pull lines:** "But there's more" and "Read on" sound like an old infomercial when spoken. Keep the open question and drop the phrase.
- **Print mechanics:** coupons, paragraph headings, serif test results, mailing lists and envelopes don't apply. The Fog Index is only a rough check on spoken lines.
- **Old offers:** the $29.95 impulse line, "call within 20 minutes" and hard-sell value stacks are dated. Use only true deadlines and real stock numbers.
- **Evidence:** his test results are his own, from print and TV in another era. Treat them as ideas to test, and never copy the 92 percent dentists example by making up a figure.
- **Cure claims:** selling the fix can't stretch to unsupported health or results claims, however well it sells.
