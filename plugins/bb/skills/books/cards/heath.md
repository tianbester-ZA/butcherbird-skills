# Made to Stick
**Chip & Dan Heath.** Read in full, 285 passages.

## The core idea
Ideas stick by design, not talent, and the enemy is knowing too much: once you know a thing, you can't imagine not knowing it. The fix is to find one core, then translate it so it is simple, unexpected, concrete, credible, emotional and told as a story. Late on, the weight moves to story: it stops the audience arguing back and puts them in a problem-solving mode.

## What this changes
- A generic script opens on a random oddity. We make the core message itself the surprise.
- A generic edit puts the stat on screen, perhaps as a chart. We lay the comparison out physically and let the figure go.
- A generic script adds a big total to back up a customer story. We leave it out, because getting people to calculate first cut giving from $2.34 to $1.26.
- A generic script promises a transformation. We show one small moment of use, seen from the viewer's own day.
- A generic script either gives everything away early or saves the point for the end. We lead with the point and keep one question open.

## The ten best passages
1. `heath-0005` · What Sticks · Even a clean chart was too rational for a ludicrous number. · Lay the real comparison out on a table beside the product, say it once, and use no graph.
2. `heath-0040` · Simple · A hook that has nothing to do with the point buries the point. · Rewrite the core until it is the hook, and cut any opener that needs a bridge back.
3. `heath-0070` · Unexpected · A good twist can't be predicted, but it makes sense in hindsight. · Plant the clue in an early shot, and aim the twist at a false assumption tied to the core.
4. `heath-0091` · Unexpected · A cold viewer has an abyss, not a curiosity gap. · Give one quick, vivid piece of context (place, person, stakes) before asking them to care.
5. `heath-0126` · Concrete · A cheap prop makes a problem feel solvable. · Hold up the product, give its cost against an everyday item, and say what it does in one sentence.
6. `heath-0161` · Credible · A claim viewers can check themselves does the convincing for you. · Build in one see-for-yourself moment, and make sure the check really proves the conclusion.
7. `heath-0170` · Emotional · Calculating first dampens feeling, so people act less. · Put no sums or spec comparisons before the emotional beat, and keep the viewer feeling through to the ask.
8. `heath-0185` · Emotional · People care about how easily they can picture a benefit, not how big it is. · Swap the big promise for a modest moment the viewer can see themselves in.
9. `heath-0238` · Stories · An argument invites a counter-argument, while a story gives the inner voice a job. · Cut the hard-sell lines and let one short story bring the viewer to the conclusion.
10. `heath-0248` · What Sticks (closing chapter) · Polish didn't predict what people remembered, but stories, emotion and a single point did. · Build the ad on one point told as a story, and don't count on a smooth presenter to make it stick.

## Three questions for the critic
1. Would the opening surprise stop making sense if another product were swapped in? (`heath-0068`)
2. Could someone who has never heard of the brand follow the set-up and care about the answer within the first few seconds? (`heath-0091`)
3. Does the draft keep every sum, spec comparison and big total away from the lead-up to the emotional beat? (`heath-0170`)

## What doesn't carry over
- **The evidence:** it comes from classrooms, labs, print, direct mail and TV. None of it measures phone video, so treat the percentages as direction, not forecasts.
- **Edit craft:** the book says almost nothing about pacing, sound or captions. Rhythm and eye are borrowed from its ideas about attention.
- **Long mysteries:** Cialdini's run over a whole lecture or 20 pages. A 15 second video has room for one question, opened and closed. Returning to it midway only suits the longer cuts.
- **Internal examples:** Commander's Intent, Southwest and the classroom exercises are aimed at staff or students who have to stay. Use them to brief the editor, not as ad formats.
- **Simplified claims:** the book prefers a simple model that can guide decisions over an accurate one nobody can use, and admits the planet model of the atom is not the real truth (`heath-0056`). That doesn't extend to product or health claims in ads.
- **Shock:** brutal Pam Laffin-style footage (`heath-0136`) and news-teaser hype don't suit brand ads.
- **Imagining:** the cable study asked homeowners to imagine themselves subscribing (`heath-0184`). Keep the viewer in the starring role, but show it through point of view and the word you rather than an instruction to imagine.
