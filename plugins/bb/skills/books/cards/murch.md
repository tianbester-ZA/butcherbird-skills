# In the Blink of an Eye
**Walter Murch.** Read in full, 124 passages.

## The core idea
A cut is a decision about what the viewer thinks and feels next, so emotion outranks story, rhythm and continuity. A good cut falls where a thought turns, as a blink does, and leads the viewer very slightly. Late in the book the argument turns to viewing conditions: image detail, screen size and a viewer who is free to leave all change how long a shot should last.

## What this changes
- A generic edit cuts to whoever is speaking. We cut on what the viewer should conclude about the line.
- A generic edit keeps cutting to stay busy. We cut when a thought changes and remove cuts that only add motion.
- A generic revision rewrites the weak section. We first check for missing setup before it.
- A generic edit protects continuity. We give up continuity first and feeling last.
- A generic edit is approved on the edit monitor. We judge pace on the full-quality export on a phone.

## The ten best passages

**`murch-0017`** · The Rule of Six
- **Lesson:** A cut is judged by emotion, story, rhythm, eye-trace, planarity and spatial continuity, in that order.
- **Do:** Name the feeling for each section. When two cuts conflict, keep the one that holds that feeling and moves the story on.

**`murch-0022`** · Misdirection
- **Lesson:** The editor stands in for the viewer and directs attention like a magician.
- **Do:** Before locking a cut, decide where the eye should be and put the key element there.

**`murch-0044`** · Test Screenings: Referred Pain
- **Lesson:** Viewers can tell you where it hurts, but not what caused it.
- **Do:** When a section feels weak, check the setup earlier in the video for missing information. Only then change the section itself.

**`murch-0053`** · Dragnet
- **Lesson:** Cutting only to the current speaker is shallow, because people look for the reaction while a line is still running.
- **Do:** Cut to a reaction, the product or B-roll mid-line, not only at the end of a sentence.

**`murch-0054`** · Dragnet
- **Lesson:** Each shot has a few natural cut points, and each one makes the viewer think something different.
- **Do:** For each claim, choose: cut away mid-line for face value, or hold on the face afterwards so it can be felt or doubted. Never cut between those two points.

**`murch-0055`** · Dragnet
- **Lesson:** Cut rate should match the rate of thought in the moment.
- **Do:** Let pace change within the video. Keep explaining sections slower and action or product sections faster.

**`murch-0056`** · Dragnet
- **Lesson:** Give the viewer what they want just before they would ask for it.
- **Do:** Show the product, price or answer a beat before the question fully forms.

**`murch-0101`** · A Galaxy of Winking Dots
- **Lesson:** Low-detail footage reads fast and tempts you to cut too soon, so the final version feels choppy.
- **Do:** Judge pace on the full-quality export, not a proxy. Lengthen any shots that now feel rushed.

**`murch-0103`** · A Galaxy of Winking Dots
- **Lesson:** A small screen among distractions has to make things collide to win attention.
- **Do:** Put the collisions in the first seconds, using a quick cut, jump cut or swish. Slow down once attention is held.

**`murch-0122`** · Near the end of the book, page not supplied
- **Lesson:** At home the viewer is king and can dismiss you at will, unlike someone who went out and committed to stay.
- **Do:** Write the opening as if the thumb is already on the swipe, with a reason to stay in the first second.

## Three questions for the critic
1. Does every key claim either cut away mid-line or hold long enough to be felt, with none stranded between the two? (`murch-0054`)
2. Does each answer, product reveal or price arrive just before the viewer would ask for it? (`murch-0056`)
3. Where the draft feels weak, is everything the viewer needs already in place before that moment? (`murch-0044`)

## What doesn't carry over
- **The cut rates.** 25 cuts a minute for action and six or fewer for dialogue are feature-film figures, and pace has risen since. Keep the logic, not the numbers (`murch-0055`, `murch-0100`).
- **The big-screen check.** Murch imagines a thousand-seat theatre and uses paper dolls to make the screen feel huge. Our final screen is the small one, so the check becomes a real phone at arm's length (`murch-0022`, `murch-0104`).
- **Theatre patience.** Slow "look-into" pacing relies on a dark room and a viewer who stays (`murch-0103`, `murch-0122`).
- **Two-person dialogue.** Many of our videos have one speaker, so the "listener" becomes a reaction, the product or B-roll. That is our adaptation, not Murch's (`murch-0053`).
- **The blink as science.** Murch's link between blinks and cuts is observation and speculation. Use it as a test of feel, not a measured rule (`murch-0050`).
- **Near-match cuts as a fixed fault.** Small jumps jar in features, yet jump cuts suit a small, distracted screen. Our reading: use them deliberately in the opening and avoid accidental ones later (`murch-0009`, `murch-0103`).
- **Feature-scale process.** The film-era machinery and the large crews of a feature don't transfer to a short phone video.
