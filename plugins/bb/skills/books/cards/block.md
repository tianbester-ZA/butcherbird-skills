# The Visual Story
**Bruce Block.** Read in full, 207 passages.

## The core idea
Everything on screen is made of space, line, shape, tone, colour, movement and rhythm, and these components are always working whether you control them or not. More difference between them raises intensity, more similarity lowers it, and that intensity should follow the story. Late on, the book applies this to commercials: any product can be made calm or wild, a short piece can hold high intensity throughout, and even 10 seconds needs visual structure.

## What this changes
- A generic edit adds energy with effects and faster cuts. We name which component changes on each cut and why.
- A generic script sets up the words and leaves the look to chance. We set the story facts and the look's rules together in the opening seconds.
- A generic grade makes the product more colourful. We make it brighter than everything around it.
- A generic brief leans on stock meanings, such as red for urgency. We teach one look its meaning by repeating it on the same beat.
- A generic cut treats movement as rhythm. We lock the sound first, then make the beats from cuts and visual change.

## The ten best passages

`block-0006` · Ch 1 · A look means what you teach it to mean, and stereotypes are the weakest option. · Use one colour or frame treatment only on the offer beat, in every reel for that brand.

`block-0090` · Ch 6 · Brightness pulls the eye before saturation, so a dark vivid pack gets ignored. · Check the product in greyscale. If it isn't the brightest area, relight or lift it in the grade.

`block-0098` · Ch 6 · Colour can be controlled by what goes in front of the camera, and a limited palette gives the colours meaning. · Put a palette rule in the shoot brief, remove wrong props and clothes, and keep the product's colour distinct.

`block-0113` · Ch 7 · Movement draws the eye first and brightness second. Eyes, vanishing points and contrast also pull attention. · In the first frames, make the thing to be seen the only mover. Remove competing movement.

`block-0124` · Ch 7 · A cut feels smooth when the focal point stays in the same screen region. · Reframe in the edit so the face or product lands in the same place on both sides of a calm cut.

`block-0132` · Ch 8 · A cut's strength comes from how different the two shots are. · Make the shots either side of a key cut differ in tone, size or colour. Don't keep returning to the same two framings.

`block-0139` · Ch 8 · Fast movement isn't rhythm, and the sound should set the visual rhythm. · Lock voice and music first, then cut to them. Don't count a busy clip as a beat.

`block-0140` · Ch 9 · Missing facts stop viewers getting involved, because they spend attention working them out. · Give who, what and why now in the opening seconds. Keep only the mystery you mean to pay off.

`block-0148` · Ch 9 · The opening sets visual rules as well as story facts. · State the palette, framing and pace with the setup, and hold them until the story turns.

`block-0178` · Ch 10 · Intensity comes from the treatment, not the product. · For a plain product, add contrast in space, line, tone, colour, movement and cutting rhythm. For a premium one, use similar shots at a slow, regular pace.

## Three questions for the critic
1. By the end of the opening seconds, does the viewer know who this is about, what the problem is and why it matters now? (`block-0140`)
2. Paused on the hook, is the thing we want seen the only mover or, if nothing moves, the brightest area? (`block-0113`)
3. At the cut into the key claim or offer, do the two shots clearly differ in tone, size or colour? (`block-0132`)

## What doesn't carry over
- **The calm opening.** The feature-film curve starts low (`block-0150`). A reel can't wait, so use the spot shape instead: spike, drop, build (`block-0160`).
- **Fifteen seconds of a complementary colour before the reveal** (`block-0093`). That can be the whole reel. Put the complement in the same frame, and treat any lead-in grade as a light touch.
- **The small-screen figures.** Screens of "an inch or two" and 21-inch televisions are dated (`block-0121`), and nothing here addresses vertical video. Its point still holds: eye jumps count for less on a small screen, so matching regions smooths a cut but won't carry the intensity.
- **Big-production control.** Painted sets, previsualisation and lens work assume a crew and a planned shoot. With creator clips, most control comes in the edit (`block-0181`, `block-0161`).
- **Film and cinema technicalities.** Film stock, lab processes, Kelvin, cinema formats and letterboxing don't apply, and open space needs a very large screen.
- **Stock shape and colour meanings.** Circles as friendly and triangles as dynamic are loose guides (`block-0067`), and the book itself warns about stereotypes (`block-0006`, `block-0138`).
- **The feed.** It says nothing about scrolling, captions or sound-off viewing, and it offers no ad results. Treat its rules as craft guidance, not tested performance.
