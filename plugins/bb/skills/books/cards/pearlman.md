# Cutting Rhythms
**Karen Pearlman.** Read in full, 258 passages.

## The core idea
Rhythm is not how often you cut. It is the movement already in the footage, shaped by timing, pacing and energy into cycles of tension and release that the viewer feels in their body. Late in the book this becomes practical: a pattern is set, then broken at the moment things change, and that break creates more intensity than short shots alone.

## What this changes
- A generic edit adds cuts to feel faster. We show only the peak of each action and pack more change into each shot.
- A generic edit keeps every shot about the same length. We set a pattern and break it on the turn.
- A generic edit cuts to the track, which hides weak cuts. We test the picture without music first.
- A generic script explains the feeling in words. We carry it in a quick, hesitant or deliberate action, or in one telling shot.
- A generic ad repeats the claim to stress it. We land it once, then move on.

## The ten best passages

`pearlman-0016` · Before Ch 1
- **Lesson:** Music can make a weak run of images seem to work.
- **Do:** Judge the cut on picture first. If it only works with the track, fix the cut.

`pearlman-0049` · Ch 2
- **Lesson:** The way an action is phrased carries a large part of its meaning without any explanation.
- **Do:** Build the product moment from clips as quick, then hesitant, then deliberate. Drop the caption that names the feeling.

`pearlman-0064` · Ch 3
- **Lesson:** More cuts can feel slower when each one shows a whole action.
- **Do:** To speed up, put several actions in one shot and keep only the peak of each.

`pearlman-0072` · Ch 3
- **Lesson:** Shots of equal length can stress very differently, because size and energy set the accent.
- **Do:** Put the price or reveal on a tight, high-energy shot. Let quiet shots rest wide.

`pearlman-0134` · Ch 8
- **Lesson:** Once a beat has landed, repeating it dulls it and stalls interest.
- **Do:** Find the second reaction, claim or product shot, and delete the weaker one.

`pearlman-0161` · Ch 9
- **Lesson:** Rhythm is only perceived through difference.
- **Do:** When an upbeat cut feels flat, change size, direction, brightness or speed across the cuts before adding more of them.

`pearlman-0191` · Ch 10
- **Lesson:** Where you cut on a face decides what the viewer thinks the person is thinking.
- **Do:** On reactions, move the cut earlier and later and rewatch. Keep the version that reads as the thought you need.

`pearlman-0201` · Ch 10
- **Lesson:** Emotion lives in the change and fades once the change is complete.
- **Do:** Slow the unboxing, pour or first fit on its way to the result. Leave soon after it lands.

`pearlman-0221` · Ch 11
- **Lesson:** A change in shot pattern marks the emotional turn, whatever form the change takes.
- **Do:** Hold one pattern through the set-up. Break it exactly on the turn, for example with a close-up, a hold, or a cut where there had been none.

`pearlman-0236` · Ch 11
- **Lesson:** Cutting before a shot is fully understood creates edge, but too much of it irritates.
- **Do:** Mix short, hard-to-read shots with shots held long enough to read, so the viewer keeps the thread.

## Three questions for the critic
1. Do the fast sections show only the peaks of actions, rather than each action from start to finish? (`pearlman-0064`)
2. Does the shot pattern change exactly on the turn or offer? (`pearlman-0221`)
3. Does each emotional beat and claim land only once? (`pearlman-0134`)

## What doesn't carry over
- **Long holds:** Ten-second shots and slow build-ups come from feature films. On a phone, a hold may need to be far shorter, because the viewer can scroll away.
- **Chase recipes:** The shot counts come from long sequences, so don't copy them as ratios. Keep the idea of staying close to the person with the most at stake.
- **Music:** The book sets music aside and treats temp tracks as a feature-film issue. Keep the mute test, but when a track or sound is the idea, the cut can follow it.
- **Dated style notes:** Average shot lengths and the remark that speed ramps look dated are old. Jump cuts need no justification now.
- **Audience claims:** "Teenage boys" and "middle-aged women" are crude groupings. Use the client's own audience and ad results.
- **Brain science:** Mirror neurons explain the author's thinking, but they aren't a test for a cut. Use the mute and tap-along checks instead.
